    Robert Pattinson’s Primetime is Grim and Unforgettable

    Give him his Oscar already.

    This review from the Venice Film Festival contains spoilers for Primetime

    It’s likely that you know how Primetime ends already. 2006: Chris Hansen and the crew of Dateline NBC’s To Catch a Predator surround the plush Texas home of an assistant district attorney named Bill Conradt. This was a bending of the TV show’s strict formula. Typically, a suburban house rigged with cameras becomes the ensnarement spot for men suspected of being pedophiles, lured there via online conversations they have with decoys with the promise of sex. Then, Hansen surprises them. Conradt was one of these men, but he wasn’t budging from his home, and so they came to him. As the local police and a camera crew entered, a gunshot rang out. Before any face-to-face conversation was had, Conradt had killed himself. 

    This was the case that essentially unraveled To Catch a Predator, a perverse show captured in perverse detail in Primetime. The movie, produced by A24, directed by Lance Oppenheim, and starring Robert Pattinson as an spookily accurate Hansen, comes two years after the release of a documentary, Predators, that questioned the intentions of the hugely popular show. It chronicled the fallout of those who had been used as decoys, publicly stung, or who worked on it. 

    All of these characters are present in Primetime too, making it an interesting, unsettlingly compulsive companion piece. Though it has fictional elements to it, with several characters given different names and conversations imagined, the film is largely based on the Esquire article “Tonight on Dateline This Man Will Die” by Luke Dittrich.

    The purring voice of a man, his face shot in extreme close-up: “I will undress you as you undress me.” It’s Hansen, rehearsing excerpts of the chat log between an online predator and a teenage girl. He treats it like Shakespeare; in a few minutes, the predator who wrote those words will be downstairs. Del, a young decoy played by Phoebe Bridgers, androgynous and experienced enough to play both men and women, welcomes a man into her “parents” house. She baked cookies; he brought condoms. Then, Hansen comes down the stairs, reciting the practised lines from the man’s chat log that landed him there. He walks out the door and is promptly body-slammed by a police officer disguised as a bush. 

    Captured on camera, Primetime presents this frenetic, compulsive formula as the magic that transformed Hansen from an Emmy Award-winning journalist into a TV shock jockey. It works, and it makes people feel good: so good, in fact, that NBC wants to use it to plug the hole left by the death of the sitcom. All of a sudden, To Catch a Predator is primetime television, going toe-to-toe with Lost in the Wednesday night 9 p.m. slot. 

    Primetime is about power, how we use it, and the culture of intimidation. In the film, that intimidation propagates from a number of angles: Skyler Gisondo plays Dan, a 23-year-old cast to play a full decade younger than his age. On his first day, he’s late onto set, arriving almost in tandem with their target. Dressed in a green polo and swim shorts, he stumbles into the kitchen, nervous, as a 30-something guy (played, in a wild piece of casting, by Jonathan Lipnicki of Stuart Little and Jerry Maguire fame) reveals his genitals to him. He did not know this was the job he signed up for.

    Meanwhile, a journalist from The National Enquirer has been stalking Hansen, capturing a photograph of him with his finger in a woman’s mouth over dinner, despite the fact he has a wife and children at home. Like his own prey, he too has been ensnared. It’s a strong enough revelation to send Hansen spinning, just as the demands of the show increase. 

    A call from the network comes: Lost now has polar bears, so they need to go bigger, or the show is chopped. A sting operator from Perverted Justice (Matthew Maher) thinks he has the answer: a big-dog government official speaking intermittently to who he thinks is a teenage boy. Suddenly, dealing with small-fry blue collar workers isn’t good enough—they need to take down a man who’ll become known as “the whale.”

    The film has a great ensemble, with Merritt Wever getting a solid side plot as Hansen’s lead producer, while Bridgers is a natural, contributing a new song to the film called “On Video.” But it’s Pattinson and Gisondo who deliver the two career-best performances. Pattinson, known better for his pomp as an actor, is sort of serial-killer sly in his mannerisms here. He captures Hansen perfectly, with his practically unflinching face and pursed lips. The voice is a straight facsimile of Hansen’s too. Gisondo, as Dan, this deadbeat actor transforming himself into, as his best friend puts it, “a nubile and hairless” pedophile’s wet dream, gets the most engaging arc. All he wants is to be in musicals (we get two renditions of “Seasons of Love”), but instead his big break comes in the form of these morally knotty, dangerous confrontations with bad people. But he’s good at it, Hansen says, and sometimes that praise and a paycheck means more than slumming it in community theater. 

    It’s a film that asks a handful of severe, somewhat terrifying questions: Is this good work? Is this noble work? And who is responsible for a dead suspected pedophile? To Catch a Predator was designed to transform hunting sex predators into a spectator sport. Is shaming someone publicly the best way to stop someone’s evil and dangerous compulsions? 

    “There used to be a pillory in every civilized town,” Pattinson’s Hansen says in the film. “Now, there’s a television in every home, and I’m on it.”

    A Slice of Cake With iKeda

    Fresh off the release of her new EP ‘Bangs and Braces,’ we take the London star on a Cheap Date.

    share

    Music

    •

    Marley Wendt

    •

    SEPTEMBER 4, 2026

    “I always have a pair of funky heels,” iKeda tells me, before pulling a pair of bright green Agent Provocateur stilettos from her bag. iKeda is, for a lack of a better word, a lot of fun. With her freshly revived sidecut and bra on display, it’s almost unfair that she’s just as nice as she is cool. 

    From touring with PinkPantheress this spring, to now releasing her EP Bangs and Braces, she embodies that type of pop stardom that is rare to catch in the flesh. Her songs are loud and punchy, smashing dancehall into electropop and pulling together the sounds she grew up with under one umbrella. “I get a lot of people being like, ‘It’s sticky club dance floor music’ or ‘music for the afters,’ but I don't actually think my music is like that,” she says. She calls it “walking around music,” which makes a lot of sense after spending two hours with her. There’s clearly a lot of world-building packed into her walks.

    Our original date plan was rained off by London weather, so we relocated to a café she warned me was giving “Temu Vibes,” drinking iced matchas and romantically sharing a strawberry cake as she told me about the whirlwind of it all. 

    MARLEY WENDT: Which three colors would you use to describe Bangs and Braces?

    IKEDA: Probably magenta and yellow, those are my favorite colors. A bit clashy, but we live! It's kind of Waluigi colors. And a mint green. 

    Is there one lyric on the EP which you feel captures the overall mood of it? 

    “It's quintessential,” because every single song on the EP has an element of quintessentiality. There’s something regal about the songs, be it “Tiger Tiger,” like the obsession with old-fashioned tea parties, or “Maps,” this feeling like you’re sitting on these old-fashioned tube lines, like the Jubilee Line that’s associated with royalty. It comes from my obsession with vintage, fine china, and things like that.

    Where did that obsession come from?

    I actually think from being obsessed with Enid Blyton! I was introduced to a lot of classical children’s books when I was young. Since I was always painting and drawing, my grandparents got me this book about a girl called Katie who visits a gallery, and she goes into all the famous paintings. There’s just a fascination with the absurdist, super-imaginative things that I’ve never really been able to let go of. Because of that, I used to want to be called Katie when I was younger. I used to hate my name.

    I used to hate my name too. 

    Really? It’s so cute though! 

    But your name is so cute too! 

    Yeah, but people were always like, “Why the fuck do you have that name?” It’s actually so random, but my mom is super hippy and she just had a dream to call the baby Ikeda. It wasn’t until, like, way later on that we found out it was a Japanese name. It’s a really common Japanese surname. 

    If you had a fragrance line, because every pop star needs a fragrance line, what would the name of the perfume be? 

    It has to be something sexy, something pyramid-inspired. Maybe Pink Pyramids. I used to be obsessed with the Nina Ricci perfumes because the bottles were everything—they were shaped like apples. I think my perfume bottle would have to be shaped like a pyramid.

    The EP obviously sits somewhere between dance hall and electropop. What drew you to bringing those worlds together?

    I’ve always struggled with combining all the different things that I’m interested in. Having a background of growing up Jamaican, but then also being really obsessed with indie music and a lot of electronic pop and stuff like that. It always used to feel quite separate growing up. On one side, I’d be listening to Vybz Kartel, and then on the other side I was listening to Paramore when I was 12. It always felt really weird to combine the two, but through doing this EP and working with Tailtiu, her just fully understanding and being into the same sort of music, and being really up for experimenting with different sounds and combining different things, I feel like that just gave me the confidence to almost make my own genre. 

    What I really like about your music is that it actually does feel very new. 

    Oh, thank you! I feel like, especially with Bangs and Braces, that it’s quite fresh. But then there’s obviously some people that are like, “Oh, this is just a derivative of stuff we had in the past. Nachos this, nachos that.” But I just feel like people don’t really know enough about music.

    In what way?

    There are other influences that inspire sounds than just specifically people. I feel like some people don’t research enough what inspired that original person.

    As much as I love pulling and drawing from nostalgia, what me and my friends always think about my music is that it’s drawn from nostalgia and things that I’ve loved growing up, but it’s transformed into something new. 

    How have you evolved as an artist with this EP?

    I’ve evolved so much as a songwriter! I always wrote short fiction growing up... 

    No way, like what?

    Oh my god, I wish I could show you some of my short fiction! It’s really cringe. Basically, I grew up quite awkward. I didn’t really get any attention from boys or anything. I was really shy and really germophobic. The idea of kissing was really scary to me. So I would fantasize about it, and I would write these short fiction books about being in school. It was always an “enemies to lovers” storyline. I was obsessed with that. 

    Did you experience it in real life? 

    No, I actually hate when guys nag me. I’ve realized that it’s the main way guys approach me. I think I dug myself into a hole from being so obsessed with that storyline when I was younger. Now I absolutely hate it. The worst thing you can ever do is nag me. I will just punch you in the face. Or I’ll cuss you out.

    Anyways, it’s crazy because for such a long time, I didn’t know how to translate that writing into my songs. I could write short fiction, but I struggled with turning it into a song, and now I feel like I’ve tapped back into that part of my brain. I just ignored the cringe and really applied it to my writing. 

    Have you ever been in a love triangle?

    [Screams] You’re going to get me in trouble. Kind of. A little pyramid…

    What’s your top love triangle?

    Definitely Twilight. I was a really big Twilight fan. But I was Team Edward.

    Thank god. I don't know if anyone is actually Team Jacob.

    [Laughs] I had some friends that were Team Jacob, but I just can’t. I just didn’t even get over the fact that he was a dog. There was something always stinky to me about werewolves. 

    You’ve also had a pretty intense run lately. How does it feel being back in London? 

    Relatively soon after I got back from the States, I went to the studio and started making new music because I was really inspired. I’m one of those people that need to learn to just enjoy things once they’re out. It’s a really annoying trait that I’ve always had. I feel like I’m now learning to just sit and enjoy.
